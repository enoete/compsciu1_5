Registers: four flip-flops that grew up

Last topic you built a thing that holds one bit. Put four of them in a row and wire each one's output into the next one's input, and something unexpected happens — the data starts to move. That one change turns storage into a conveyor belt, and conveyor belts turn out to be how your keyboard talks to your computer, how a processor counts, and how every instruction your machine has ever run got fetched. Push the button and watch a bit walk.

A 4-bit shift register

Set the serial input, then pulse the clock. Every bit steps one place to the right, and whatever falls off the end is gone.

Why bother moving data one bit at a time?

It looks obviously worse. Four bits at once beats one bit four times over, every time. So why does almost every cable you own send data serially?

Count the wires in a USB cable

Your laptop moves data 64 bits at a time internally. If your USB cable worked the same way it would need 64 separate wires inside it, all exactly the same length, all perfectly in step, for two metres. That cable would be as thick as your wrist, cost a fortune, and stop working if one strand stretched slightly. So instead the cable has one data wire and sends the bits one after another, very fast. Something at each end converts between the two. That converter is a shift register, and it is why the cable in your bag is thinner than a pencil.

This is the single most useful thing in the topic. Parallel is fast but expensive in wires. Serial is cheap in wires but takes more clock cycles. A shift register is the translator between the two worlds, which is why SIPO (serial in, parallel out) and PISO (parallel in, serial out) exist at all. If an exam asks why shift registers matter, that sentence is the answer.
Where the conversion is happening right now. Your keyboard sends each keypress to the computer serially down a couple of wires; a SIPO register at the other end reassembles it into a parallel byte. Ethernet, SATA, HDMI and USB all do the same. Even inside a chip, sending 64 signals across the silicon costs real area, so designers convert to serial for long runs and back to parallel at the destination.

The four types, side by side

All four are the same four flip-flops. The only thing that changes is which doors are open — how data gets in, and how it gets out. Two choices, two positions each, four combinations. That is the whole naming scheme.

A cinema with two kinds of door

People can come in through one turnstile, single file, or through a bank of doors all at once. Same for leaving. Nobody changes the seats — only the doors. A turnstile in and a turnstile out is SISO. Turnstile in, wide doors out is SIPO. Wide doors in, turnstile out is PISO. Wide doors both ends is PIPO, and it is the fastest by a mile because nobody queues. Read the four letters and you already know which doors are open.

The mistake that costs whole questions. A serial register needs one clock pulse per bit. Loading four bits serially takes four pulses; a parallel load takes one. So when a question says "how many clock cycles to load an 8-bit value", the answer is 8 for serial input and 1 for parallel input — and the same logic applies to getting data back out. Count the bits, count the pulses.

The universal shift register

One register that can do all of it: load in parallel, shift right, shift left, or sit still. Two control lines pick the mode, and a multiplexer on each bit does the choosing — the same MUX you built in topic 3, finally doing a real job.

The gear stick

A car does not have four separate engines for park, forward, reverse and neutral. It has one engine and a gear selector. A universal shift register is the same idea: one set of flip-flops, and a two-bit selector that decides whether this clock pulse means load, shift right, shift left or hold. Change the gear, not the engine.

Four choices per bit means a 4-to-1 multiplexer per bit. That is it. That is the entire design. A 4-bit universal shift register is four D flip-flops and four 4:1 multiplexers, with S1 and S0 wired to every multiplexer's select lines at once. If you can draw one bit of it, you can draw all four.
Shifting left multiplies by two. Shifting right divides by two. Try it above: load 0011 (three), shift left, and you get 0110 (six). Shift left again for 1100 (twelve). Processors use this constantly because shifting is enormously cheaper than real multiplication — if a program multiplies by 8, the compiler quietly turns it into three left shifts and nobody mentions it.

Counters: a register that counts itself

Same four flip-flops one more time, wired a third way. Nothing shifts here — every clock pulse the whole register just adds one to itself. There are two ways to build that, and the gap between them is one of the most commonly examined ideas in this part of the syllabus.

The odometer in a car

Watch the mile counter on an old mechanical odometer. The units wheel ticks over on every mile. Only when it completes a full turn — rolling from 9 back to 0 — does it nudge the tens wheel one notch. The tens wheel never watches the car; it only watches the units wheel sitting next to it. That is exactly how a ripple counter works, and it is exactly why it has a weakness too: the tens wheel cannot move until the units wheel has finished moving, so for a brief moment while 999 becomes 1000, the display can show nonsense in between.

Build it: the asynchronous (ripple) counter

Four flip-flops wired as simple toggles — tie T permanently to 1 and a flip-flop flips on every clock edge it receives. The trick is the clock itself: only FF0 gets the real clock. Every stage after that is clocked by the Q output of the flip-flop before it.

Nothing here is a shared bus — follow FF0's Q into FF1's clock input, FF1's Q into FF2's, and so on. That chain is the entire circuit.

Why “asynchronous”? Nothing here shares one clock line. FF1 only moves once FF0's output has already changed, and that change takes a small but real amount of time (gate delay) to happen. Watch it below: turning 0111 into 1000 means every single bit has to flip, and they do it one after another, not all at once.

Watch the worst-case ripple

0111 (seven) is about to become 1000 (eight) — every single bit has to change. Step through it one gate-delay at a time and watch what the register reads in between.

Build it: the synchronous counter

Same four toggle flip-flops, but every CLK pin is wired to the same clock line. To still count correctly, each stage now needs to know in advance whether it is allowed to toggle this pulse — so an AND gate feeds every T input after the first, enabled only when every bit before it is already 1.

FF1 toggles whenever Q0 is 1. FF2 toggles only when Q0 and Q1 are both 1. FF3 toggles only when Q0, Q1 and Q2 are all 1 — built from two cascaded 2-input ANDs rather than one 3-input gate.

No ripple, no glitch. Every flip-flop sees the clock edge at the same instant. The AND gates have already worked out, before the clock even ticks, exactly which bits are allowed to flip — so 0111 jumps straight to 1000 in one clean step. The price is the gates themselves: more hardware to do the same job.

The registers with names

Inside a processor there are registers doing specific jobs, and each one has a name you are expected to know. They are not special hardware — every one of them is a row of flip-flops exactly like the one you just built. What makes them different is what they are wired to — and once you notice that every single one of them holds exactly one kind of thing, the list below stops being eight names to memorise and becomes a handful of ideas, each with one or two registers attached. They're grouped and colour-coded by that below.

A chef working from a recipe book

The book is on a stand, open at a page. Your finger marks which line you are on — that is the program counter. You read that line out loud and hold it in your head while you work — the instruction register. The ingredient you are currently chopping sits on the board in front of you — the accumulator. When you need something from the pantry you first note down which shelf — the memory address register — and what comes back sits in the bowl by the door — the memory data register. Nobody gets a bigger kitchen. Each thing just has its own place, so you never have to wonder where anything is.

Click any register — it opens right there to tell you more.

MDR and MBR — the pair everyone muddles. The Memory Address Register holds where. The Memory Data Register holds what. Some textbooks also call the MDR a Memory Buffer Register — they are the same thing under two names, and CAPE papers use both. If a question mentions both MDR and MBR as separate items, treat MBR as the buffer sitting on the bus and MDR as the register the CPU reads from; say in one line that many designs merge them, and you will not lose the mark either way.

Watch them work together

This is what all of it was for. Before you press anything, know what you are actually watching — a tiny program is about to run, and it is doing something as ordinary as ringing up a sale.

Ringing up a sale, one line at a time

The five lines sitting in memory below are a program that, in plain English, says: start the sale at $5, add a $3 item, save that subtotal on the receipt, then take $2 off with a coupon. Final total: $6. Once you press Next step, watch the accumulator and you are watching the till display update. Watch memory address 0x07 and you are watching the receipt get written.

The CPU cannot read those four sentences in one go — it cannot read at all, really. So for every single line it repeats the same three-step routine: fetch that line off the till roll, decode what it is asking for, execute it, then go back for the next line. It has done nothing else since the day it was switched on, several billion times a second, and it will keep doing nothing else until it is switched off. Every app, every game, every video call is that loop running very fast over a very long list.

Main memory

Registers

Notice what the program counter does, and when. It is incremented during the fetch, not at the end of the instruction. By the time the CPU is executing the current instruction, the PC is already pointing at the next one. That is why a jump instruction works by overwriting the PC — it is simply telling the finger to move to a different line of the recipe.

Three rules before you place a single component

  1. PRE′ and CLR′ go to toggle switches, switched ON. Every flip-flop, every time. They are active low, so a 1 means "leave me alone". A flip-flop that ignores everything you do is almost always one of these sitting at 0.
  2. All CLK pins connect to the SAME clock source. One button, wired to all four. If each flip-flop gets its own button you have built four separate flip-flops that happen to be near each other, not a register.
  3. Bit order matters on the display. Q0 is the least significant bit and goes to display input A. Q3 is the most significant and goes to input D. Get this backwards and your decimal readout will be nonsense while your LEDs look perfectly fine.
